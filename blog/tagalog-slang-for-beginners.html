How to Pick Up Tagalog Slang (Without Getting Lost)

Tagalog slang can be tricky, but you can use it naturally with the right tips. Here’s how to understand and try out Filipino slang words without getting lost.

Tagalog slang is everywhere—family parties, group chats, even memes—but it can feel like a secret code if you didn’t grow up speaking it. The good news: you don’t need to be fluent to start understanding or even using common Filipino slang. Here’s how to pick up the words that actually get used, so you don’t get lost in the conversation.

Get to Know the Most Common Tagalog Slang Words

If you want to sound natural in casual Tagalog, start by noticing the words that pop up everywhere in daily conversation and on social media. For example, you’ll often hear “bes” used for best friend. Someone might say, “Bes, tara na!” (“Bestie, let’s go!”) when inviting a close friend out. Another favorite is “petmalu”, which is a playful way to say malupit (awesome or impressive), created by reversing the syllables—a style known as swardspeak. If someone does something cool, you might hear, “Petmalu ka talaga!” (“You’re really awesome!”) And if you ever see “lodi”, that’s “idol” spelled backward—used to refer to someone you admire.

Tagalog slang also loves to blend English and Filipino. Take “keri” (from “carry”), which means “I can handle it” or “It’s okay.” You might hear, “Keri ko ‘to!” (“I can handle this!”) Or there’s “walwal”, which means to party hard or go out drinking. For example: “Nag-walwal kami kagabi.” (“We partied hard last night.”)

Some slang words come from flipping syllables around, a process popularized in swardspeak. “Jowa” is a great example—it means boyfriend or girlfriend, and is thought to be derived from “asawa” (spouse or partner), though the exact origin is debated. If you hear a word that sounds odd, try saying it backward or rearranging the syllables; it might just reveal its original meaning!

Learn from Real Conversations—Not Just Lists

Textbooks rarely capture how Tagalog slang really sounds in the wild. For a more authentic feel, immerse yourself in real conversations. Watching Filipino YouTubers, TikTok creators, or listening to local podcasts lets you hear slang as it’s actually used. You'll notice how casual phrases pop up naturally, with the right emotion and timing. For example, hearing someone say, "Grabe, ang saya dito!" (Wow, it’s so fun here!) carries more punch than just reading the word grabe on a list.

Family group chats or lively party conversations are also treasure troves for picking up slang. Pay attention to what makes people laugh or react strongly—chances are, there’s a slang term at the center of it. If someone in your group says, "Petmalu ka talaga!" (You’re really amazing!), you know it’s a playful twist of words that you’d rarely find in formal lessons.

  • Observe: When you hear a new word, watch how people use it. Is it a joke, a compliment, or maybe teasing?
  • Be curious: Don’t hesitate to ask, "Ano ibig sabihin nun?" (What does that mean?) Most people enjoy explaining slang—it’s a fun way to share culture.
  • Look it up: If you’re shy, look up the word later. You’ll often find examples that help you remember when and how to use it.

By learning from real conversations, you’ll pick up natural expressions, sound more authentic, and feel more at home in any Filipino crowd.

Try Using Slang—But Start Small

Once you've heard a few Tagalog slang words in action, try picking just one or two favorites to use now and then—even in your English sentences. This is a low-pressure way to get comfortable and see how these words work. For example, you might say, “Ang saya ng party, bes!” (The party was so fun, bestie!). Dropping in a word like bes (bestie) or petmalu (cool or awesome) can help you ease into using slang without feeling overwhelmed.

Start by using slang with people you already know well, like cousins or close friends. They’ll usually find it fun, and they’ll let you know if your usage sounds natural—or if it’s a little off. Most Filipinos enjoy seeing others try out their language, and friends are happy to help you get it right.

Remember, there’s no rush. If you’re not sure how or when to use a slang word, it’s perfectly fine to just listen. Notice how friends or family slip these words into conversation. Over time, you’ll get a feel for which slang fits in a certain situation and which are best left for later. The goal isn’t to sound like a native right away—it’s to join in and have fun with the language, one word at a time.

Avoid Common Mistakes and Sound Natural

When you’re adding Tagalog slang to your conversations, subtlety goes a long way. It’s tempting to sprinkle every new word you learn into a sentence, but using too much slang all at once can feel forced. Instead, work in just one or two familiar expressions—this makes you sound more natural and relaxed. For example, saying "Astig! Ang ganda ng jacket mo." ("Cool! Your jacket looks great.") feels effortless, while cramming in several slang phrases might confuse your listener.

Don’t stress about having a perfect accent. Focus on saying words clearly, with each vowel sounded out, and listen carefully to how native speakers pronounce them. Tagalog is a language where clear pronunciation matters more than hitting every subtle sound perfectly. If you hear someone say "G na!" (“Let’s go!”), try repeating it in the same easy, casual way. Over time, your ear and mouth will adjust naturally.

Be aware that some slang is very regional or tied to certain age groups. If you use a word and your tita (aunt) looks puzzled, that’s a sign it might not be widely understood. It’s safest to stick with slang you’ve heard in different settings or from several people. If you’re ever unsure, you can always ask, “Ano ibig sabihin nun?” (“What does that mean?”) and learn even more in the process.

Tools and Tricks to Keep Learning

Staying on top of Tagalog slang is a lot easier with the right habits and tools. Here are some practical ways to keep learning and make new words stick:

  • Slip Tagalog into your routine. Chrome extensions built for language learners can sprinkle Tagalog words, slang included, into your daily browsing. You'll catch phrases like lodi (idol, someone you admire) or petmalu (really amazing or cool) right where you least expect them, making review effortless. (Disclosure: I built Hari for this—it's a Chrome extension that swaps bits of English for Tagalog while you browse, with instant translations and pronunciation.)
  • Keep a running list. Every time you hear a new slang word, jot it down—old-school notebook, phone notes, whatever works. Add the meaning and a sample sentence for context. For example: Jeproks (laid-back, relaxed). Sentence: Si Mark ay jeproks lang kahit may problema. (Mark is just laid-back even when there are problems.) This habit builds your own mini-dictionary, making words harder to forget.
  • Follow where the slang lives. Filipino meme pages, comedy accounts, and comment sections are like living slang dictionaries. You’ll see real phrases in their natural habitat—sometimes before they hit mainstream TV or movies. Plus, you get the bonus of seeing how each term is actually used in a conversation or a joke, not just on a list.

Try combining these tricks to get a steady stream of fresh slang, all wrapped into the habits you already have. The more you notice and collect, the more natural your Tagalog will sound.

Keep It Fun and Stay Curious

Keep It Fun and Stay Curious

Learning Tagalog slang doesn’t have to feel overwhelming. The most important thing is to keep your curiosity alive and make the process enjoyable. You don’t need to memorize long lists or use every new word right away. Instead, focus on picking up a few key slang words, listening closely to how they’re used, and slowly adding them into your conversations. For example, if you hear someone say, "Ang saya dito!" (It’s so fun here!), notice how saya (fun/joy) pops up in casual talk.

Remember, real-life exposure is the best teacher. Chatting with friends, watching short videos, or reading comments online can help you notice how slang fits naturally into daily language. Don’t worry if you make mistakes—Tagalog speakers usually appreciate your effort and sense of humor. Just keep experimenting, asking questions, and trying out new phrases when you feel comfortable.

If you want to fit Tagalog into your routine, browsing-based exposure—like reading stories, posts, or even using tools such as Hari—can help you see slang in action without extra pressure. Every new word is a small win, and every conversation is a chance to learn something new. Stay playful and curious, and you’ll find yourself understanding and using Tagalog slang with more confidence over time.
