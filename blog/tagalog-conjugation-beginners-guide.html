Tagalog Conjugation: A Beginner's Guide to Making Sentences

Confused by Tagalog verbs? This guide breaks down the basics—focus, aspect, and simple hacks—so you can start making real sentences, not just memorizing charts.

Tagalog verb conjugation looks scary, but you don’t need a grammar degree to get started. This guide shows how Tagalog verbs change (focus, aspect, actor vs. object), with simple examples and hacks. If you understand some Tagalog but freeze when you try to speak, you’re in the right place.

What Is Tagalog Verb Conjugation?

Conjugation is just a fancy word for changing a verb to show when something happens—like the past, present, or future. In English, you might say "eat," "ate," or "will eat" to show the time. Tagalog verbs work the same way, but there’s another twist: the verb changes can also show what the main focus of the sentence is. Sometimes the action itself is the focus, sometimes the person doing it, and sometimes the thing being acted on.

This means Tagalog verbs can look very different depending on what you want to say. For example, the verb for "to eat" is kain. If you want to say "is eating" with the action as the focus, you might say kumakain. If you want to put the focus on the object being eaten, you might use kinakain. Both are correct, but the meaning shifts a little depending on what you want to highlight.

  • Kumakain ako. (I am eating.)
  • Kinakain ko ang mansanas. (I am eating the apple.)

You’ll often see prefixes like mag-, nag-, and infixes like um-, in-. Sometimes pieces get added into the middle or end of the verb, too. At first, this can look confusing, but don’t stress about every rule yet. Focus on the patterns you hear most often in conversation, and with practice, these forms will start to make sense.

Focus: Actor vs. Object

One of the most important things to understand about Tagalog verbs is focus. Focus tells you whether the sentence highlights the person doing the action (actor focus) or the thing affected by the action (object focus).

Actor Focus

In actor focus, the doer of the action is the main point. For example:

  • Kumain ako. (I ate.)

Here, "ako" (I) is the focus, and the verb uses the "um-" infix. Other common actor focus prefixes are mag- and nag-. These forms are used when you want to make it clear that who did the action is most important.

Object Focus

In object focus, the thing being acted on is in the spotlight. For example:

  • Kinain ko ang mansanas. (I ate the apple.)

In this sentence, "ang mansanas" (the apple) is the focus. The verb uses the "in-" infix. Other common object focus affixes are i- and -an. These forms put the object, not the doer, at the center.

Understanding focus is key to making clear sentences in Tagalog. The choice between actor and object focus changes the verb and tells your listener what part of the sentence matters most.

Aspect: Past, Present, Future (Not Tense!)

When learning Tagalog verbs, it’s important to know that Tagalog focuses on aspect instead of tense. In English, verbs change to show whether something happens in the past, present, or future. In Tagalog, verbs show whether an action is completed, ongoing, or not yet started. This is called aspect. Think of it as focusing on whether the action is finished, happening now, or still to come.

  • Past/Completed Aspect: This shows an action that is finished. Common markers are nag-, um-, and in-. For example: Nagbasa siya. (He/She read.)
  • Present/Ongoing Aspect: This is used for actions that are happening now. You’ll often see reduplication of part of the verb root with nag-, um-, or in-. For example: Nagbabasa siya. (He/She is reading.)
  • Future/Contemplated Aspect: This is for actions that have not started yet. Markers include mag-, ma-, and i-. For example: Magbabasa siya. (He/She will read.)

Notice how the verb changes slightly for each aspect, but the sentence pattern stays similar. Understanding aspect helps you talk about when things happen in Tagalog, even though the language doesn’t use tense in the same way as English. Practice listening to native speakers and pay attention to the verb forms—they tell you a lot about the timing of the action.

Easy Hacks for Building Sentences

When you’re just starting to make Tagalog sentences, the actor focus is your best friend. These are sentences where the person doing the action is the focus. For beginners, mag- verbs are the easiest place to start. For example: Magluto ako. (I will cook.) It’s just the verb plus the pronoun. No extra words needed!

Many everyday actions use mag- verbs. Here are a few basics you’ll use all the time:

  • mag-aral – to study (Mag-aral ka. – You (should) study.)
  • magtimpla – to mix/make a drink (Magtimpla siya. – He/She will make a drink.)
  • maglaba – to do laundry (Maglaba tayo. – Let’s do laundry.)

Get to know the basic pronouns to build simple sentences:

  • ako – I, me
  • ka/ikaw – you (informal)
  • siya – he, she
  • kami – we (not including you)
  • tayo – we (including you)
  • sila – they

With these building blocks—mag- verbs and pronouns—you can start making lots of useful sentences. Try switching out the pronouns to practice:

  • Mag-aral ako. (I will study.)
  • Maglaba sila. (They will do laundry.)

Learning Without Memorizing Charts

If memorizing verb charts feels overwhelming, you’re not alone. A more natural way to learn Tagalog verbs is to notice them in real life. Listen to family conversations, watch TV shows, or check out YouTube vlogs in Tagalog. Pay attention to the verbs people use and the way they change depending on what’s happening. You’ll start to notice patterns, even if you don’t know all the rules yet.

Another practical tip: try swapping just one English verb for a Tagalog one in your daily sentences. For example, instead of saying, "I will drive to work," say, Mag-drive ako to work ("I will drive to work"). This is Taglish—mixing Tagalog and English—which is common in casual speech. Don’t worry if the rest of your sentence is in English at first—this helps you get comfortable using verbs in context, and it’s how many Filipinos speak casually anyway. (Note: 'mag-drive' is a Tagalized English verb, which is common in spoken Tagalog.)

It also helps to see verbs in real sentences while you read or browse. Tools designed for language learners can highlight verbs as you encounter them, so you see how they’re really used. This is much more memorable than drilling long lists. The more you notice verbs in action, the more you’ll start to pick up the patterns on your own—without ever needing to memorize a full chart.

You Don’t Need to Master Every Rule—Just Start Talking

You Don’t Need to Master Every Rule—Just Start Talking

As you begin your journey with Tagalog verbs, remember: you don’t need to memorize every chart or rule before you can speak. Most Filipinos use a small set of verbs and patterns in daily life, and you can too. Focus on understanding the core ideas: actor vs. object focus, and how verbs change to show past, present, or future actions. With just a handful of common verbs and sentence patterns, you can express a surprising amount.

For example, notice how these simple sentences work:

  • Kumain ako. (I ate.)
  • Kakain siya. (He/She will eat.)
  • Binili nila ang gulay. (They bought the vegetables.)

Don’t worry if you mix up forms sometimes—native speakers will still understand you. The most important step is to start using Tagalog in real conversations, even if it’s just a few words at first. The more you use it, the more natural it will feel.

Finally, fitting Tagalog into your daily routine helps a lot. Reading short Tagalog phrases while browsing, like with Hari, is an easy way to make the language part of your everyday life. (Disclosure: I built Hari for this exact reason.) The more you see and hear Tagalog, the more familiar sentences and verb forms will become. Celebrate small wins and keep talking—progress will come with practice.
