The Easiest Tagalog Words for English Speakers to Learn First

Start your Tagalog journey with words that are easy for English speakers to pick up—loanwords, cognates, and everyday basics. Build confidence with these simple wins.

Some Tagalog words are much easier for English speakers to learn than others. If you focus on words that sound like English (loanwords and cognates), plus a few must-know basics, you can start speaking Tagalog faster and feel more confident. Here’s a guide to the simplest Tagalog words to get you going, especially if you’re a heritage learner or total beginner.

Loanwords: Tagalog Words That Came From English

One of the friendliest surprises for English speakers starting Tagalog is how many words are borrowed straight from English. You’ll hear words like kompyuter (computer), prinsipal (principal), and basketbol (basketball) in daily conversation, and they work almost exactly as they do in English—just said with a Tagalog accent.

Many everyday objects keep their English names, often with a Tagalog twist. For example, although silya (from Spanish) is the formal word for “chair,” most people say upuan for "chair" in daily conversation, but "chair" is also understood, especially in Taglish. You’ll also hear mesa (from Spanish) for “table,” and telepono for “telephone,” which is borrowed from Spanish and pronounced a bit differently.

Even verbs sometimes come straight from English, especially in informal situations. For instance:

  • Magda-drive ako. (I will drive.)
  • Nagtetext siya. (He/She is texting.)

These borrowed words make Tagalog feel more approachable. As you listen and practice, you’ll notice that many English words pop up in daily conversations—sometimes mixed with Tagalog grammar. This can help you pick up new vocabulary quickly and feel more comfortable using Tagalog right away.

Cognates: Words That Sound and Mean the Same

Cognates are words that look and sound almost the same in both Tagalog and English, making them easy for beginners to spot and remember. These familiar words can help you start building your confidence right away. For example, doktor means "doctor" (from Spanish), banka (from Spanish "banca") means "small boat," and direksiyon means "direction" (from Spanish "dirección"). You’ll find these words used in everyday conversation, and they usually stand out clearly, even in longer sentences.

Numbers are another area where English speakers have a head start. The Spanish numbers—uno, dos, tres—are still common, especially when counting out loud or telling the time. At the same time, English numbers are mixed in all the time in daily life. For example, someone might say, "Magkano ito? Ten pesos." ("How much is this? Ten pesos.") or "Alas dos na." ("It's two o'clock.")

  • Uno – one
  • Dos – two
  • Tres – three

Days of the week and months are also very easy to recognize, since most are borrowed from Spanish and sound almost exactly like their English or Spanish counterparts. For example, Lunes means "Monday," and Mayo means "May." These words are used everywhere—from calendars to news and casual conversation—so you'll get plenty of practice with them.

Everyday Basics: Words You’ll Hear All the Time

When you’re just starting out with Tagalog, focusing on words you’ll actually hear and use every day makes it easier to remember them. Start with greetings and polite expressions that instantly make a good impression. For example:

  • salamat – thank you
  • opo – yes (polite, often used with elders)
  • kumusta – how are you?

Family terms are at the heart of life, especially in Filipino-American households. You’ll hear these words all the time, and using them yourself shows care and respect:

  • lola – grandma
  • tita – aunt
  • kuya – older brother (or respectful way to address an older male)
  • ate – older sister (or respectful way to address an older female)

Some other basic words pop up everywhere in daily conversation:

  • tubig – water
  • kain – eat (verb root, as in “Let’s eat!”)
  • bahay – house
  • gutom – hungry

Even if you just learn these few words, you’ll already be able to greet someone, show respect, and handle the essentials of daily life.

Short, Simple Words You Already Know

Some of the most helpful Tagalog words for beginners are short, simple, and used every day. Words like oo (yes), hindi (no), saan (where), and paano (how) are easy to remember and say. These are the building blocks of thousands of questions and answers in daily conversation.

Try practicing with common questions you’ll hear from friends and family. For example:

  • Saan ka? (Where are you?)
  • Kumain ka na? (Have you eaten?)

Even with just these short words and basic phrases, you can already understand and take part in simple chats. You’ll hear oo and hindi all the time as people answer questions or agree and disagree. Likewise, saan and paano are used to start questions about places and ways of doing things.

Getting comfortable with these small but powerful words will make listening and speaking feel much more natural. As you watch TV shows, listen to conversations, or chat with friends, you’ll notice these words popping up again and again. The more you hear and use them, the more confident you’ll become with Tagalog basics.

Tips for Building Your Early Tagalog Vocabulary

Building your early Tagalog vocabulary works best when you start with what feels familiar. If you have Filipino family or friends, listen closely to their conversations—even when they switch between Tagalog and English. You’ll pick up words that are truly used every day, like kuya (older brother), ate (older sister), or salamat (thank you). TV shows and movies are another great place to catch common words in context, often mixed with English. Don’t worry about understanding everything at once; just notice the words that come up often.

Another practical tip is to look around your home and make a list of everyday items—like "door," "table," or "water." Then, look up their Tagalog names and try to use them as you go about your routine. For example, say pinto for "door," mesa for "table" (from Spanish), and tubig for "water." Labeling things or simply saying the Tagalog word before you use or touch the item can help you remember it.

If you like picking up new words as you browse online, there are tools designed to help you do just that. Some extensions or features can swap in easy Tagalog words on your favorite sites, letting you learn naturally as you read. This way, you build your vocabulary with almost no extra effort—and you’ll start to recognize Tagalog words out in the wild, too.

Start Small and Celebrate Every Win

Start Small and Celebrate Every Win

Building your Tagalog vocabulary doesn’t have to feel overwhelming. Focusing on loanwords, familiar-sounding cognates, and the most useful everyday phrases gives you a strong and practical foundation. Even learning just a handful of words like salamat (thank you), opo (polite yes, with respect), or asul (blue, from Spanish "azul") opens up real-life connections and little moments of understanding. Remember, every new word you pick up is a step toward real conversation.

Staying consistent is more important than cramming. A few minutes each day—whether it’s reviewing flashcards, hearing a word in context, or repeating phrases you hear—will build your confidence and help the words stick. Celebrate each word you learn; those small victories add up.

Fitting Tagalog into your daily life is easier than you might think. You could label common items at home, greet friends in Tagalog, or simply pause to notice familiar words in Filipino media. Even browsing-based tools, like Hari (I built a thing for this), can help you weave Tagalog into your regular reading without extra effort. The more you see and use these easy words, the more natural they become.

As you keep going, remember that language learning is a journey. Start small, enjoy the process, and give yourself credit for every win, no matter how simple it seems. Those early words are the building blocks for everything else to come.
