Our Philosophy

Immersion is how you actually
learn a language

Hari is built on Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis — one of the most replicated findings in second-language acquisition research. The core insight: you acquire language when you receive input that is just one step beyond your current level (i+1), embedded in context you already understand. It's why living abroad works faster than any class, and why Duolingo streaks don't. Hari delivers that same mechanism inside the tabs you already have open.

Krashen's i+1 in practice

Hari swaps individual English words for Tagalog equivalents inside sentences you already understand. The surrounding context carries the meaning — your brain acquires the word without ever drilling it.

Incidental acquisition at scale

Research consistently shows incidental vocabulary exposure — encountering words repeatedly in natural context — outperforms deliberate study for long-term retention. Hari replicates this across every article, thread, and feed you read.

Low affective filter

Krashen's Affective Filter Hypothesis: anxiety blocks acquisition. No quizzes, no failure states, no streak pressure. Hari keeps the learning environment stress-free — which is exactly when your brain absorbs language most efficiently.

A few questions, answered

Most users start recognizing individual words within the first few weeks of regular browsing. Understanding phrases and following family conversations takes longer — typically a few months of consistent daily exposure. Immersion builds receptive vocabulary gradually; the gains are real but not overnight. If you browse an hour or two a day, you're getting meaningful Tagalog exposure you simply weren't getting before.

Honest answer: not on its own. Hari builds strong receptive skills — listening and reading comprehension, vocabulary recognition, a feel for natural Tagalog. But speaking fluency requires output practice: actual conversation, ideally with native speakers (italki, a language partner, or your family). Think of Hari as doing the heavy lifting on comprehension so that when you do speak, you already have a vocabulary to draw from.

Receptive immersion — reading and listening — can realistically take you to solid intermediate comprehension (roughly A2–B1 on the CEFR scale) with consistent daily exposure over 6–12 months. You'll understand the gist of family conversations, follow most of a Filipino movie without subtitles, and read Tagalog social media posts. Getting to B2 and above requires pairing immersion with active output practice. Hari is exceptional at the receptive half of that equation.

Duolingo's Tagalog course is thin — the vocabulary ceiling is low and sentences are artificial. Anki is excellent for drilling cards, but retrieval practice alone isn't the same as encountering words in natural context repeatedly. Hari works in the hours you're already browsing — no extra time blocked off. The two approaches complement each other: Anki can help you memorize a word, and Hari gives you the natural-context repetitions that actually cement it long-term.
